Period doubling and the bifurcation diagram

Iterate a one-hump map such as x ↦ 1 − μx² and record where the orbit settles. As μ grows, the single fixed point splits into a 2-cycle, then a 4-cycle, then 8, 16, … The doublings come faster and faster and pile up at μ∞, where chaos begins.

Drag to zoom into a region · double-click to reset

Self-similarity. "Zoom one level deeper" shrinks the window around μ∞ by δ horizontally and by α vertically, flipping it each time, and the same picture comes back. Those two zoom factors are the Feigenbaum constants. For the quadratic hump they are δ ≈ 4.669 and α ≈ 2.503. A flatter hump (x⁴, x⁶, x⁸) gives different constants, and that is the "degree" in this project.

δ: how fast the doublings arrive

Let μn be the parameter where the 2n-cycle is most stable. The gaps between successive μn shrink geometrically. Their ratio converges to δ, and the limit is the same for every smooth map with the same kind of hump. That universality is Feigenbaum's discovery (1978).

Ratios (μn − μn−1)/(μn+1 − μn) computed from the actual maps, against our high-precision δ (dashed). Hover for values.

α and the renormalisation operator

Why is there a universal number at all? Look at the map applied twice, g∘g, near its top: it is again a one-hump map, upside down and smaller. Rescale it back up and you get a new map,

(𝒯g)(x) = g(g(λx)) / λ,   λ = g(1).

Doubling the period of g is the same as an ordinary bifurcation of 𝒯g, so applying 𝒯 is "zooming in one level". At μ∞ you can zoom forever, so repeated applications of 𝒯 converge to a fixed point, the Feigenbaum function g = 𝒯g. Then α = −1/g(1). And δ is the rate at which 𝒯 pushes you away from g when you start slightly off μ∞.

Start from f(x) = 1 − μ|x|z at μ = μ∞ and apply 𝒯 repeatedly: the curves collapse onto the fixed point g (dashed). Nudge the start off μ∞ and they follow g for a while, then peel away. Each application of 𝒯 multiplies the offset by δ, so a 10× smaller nudge buys about log(10)/log(δ) ≈ 1.5 more steps.

The fixed points themselves. A flatter top (higher degree) gives a different g, and so a different α = −1/g(1) and δ.

For the classical constants, Molteni (2016) reported about 10,000 digits but did not publish them. The largest public table (Broadhurst, 1999) had 1,018 or 1,019 digits. "Proven" means rigorously certified by a computer-assisted proof. All our digits agree with every proven digit.

How it was computed

Chebyshev collocation. Write the fixed point as a sum of Chebyshev polynomials in t = |x|z, and require the equation g(1)·g(x) = g(g(g(1)·x)) to hold exactly at n carefully chosen points. That gives n equations in n unknowns, solved by Newton's method in 34,000-bit arithmetic. δ is the leading eigenvalue of 𝒯 linearised at g, and is solved the same way. This extends Molteni's method (2016) from the quadratic case to any even degree.

Why it converges so fast. The Feigenbaum function is analytic, so its Chebyshev coefficients decay geometrically. Each extra node buys a fixed number of digits: about 1.6 for the quadratic case and 0.7 for degree 8. 6,514 nodes gives 10,000 digits.

Verification

The three checks.

  1. Every value was computed twice, with different node counts (n and ≈1.1n) and different precisions. The published digits stop 10 short of where the two agree.
  2. A second, independent implementation agrees on every digit it computed. It uses Legendre polynomials, different nodes, Arb ball arithmetic and full Newton steps, and shares no code with the first.
  3. The results agree with every previously published digit, including the 325 rigorously proven digits of δ for degree 4. Simulating period doubling in the maps directly reproduces each δ to 7–12 digits.

These are computed digits, not a proof.
